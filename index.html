Web Design (Desktop and Mobile)

Your website is where every other channel sends people. Search, display, email and social all end with a click to a page, and that page decides whether the click turns into a customer. This chapter is about designing pages that convert, and testing them so you know they do.

03

The big picture

Five web design frameworks

These aren't competing theories. Each one is a different lens on the same page. Good designers run a page through all five, like a checklist.

1 · Design for usability

Can people use the site without thinking about it?

2 · Conversion-centered design

Does every part of the page push toward one goal?

3 · Three questions

Can a visitor tell what you offer, why you, and what to do next?

4 · Segments

Does the page work for every type of shopper who lands on it?

5 · Mobile first

Was the page designed for the smallest screen, then built up?
Design is a conversion lever, not decoration. Every choice on a page (a headline, a button, a photo) is something you can test and measure.
Framework 1

Design for usability

People don't read web pages; they scan them. A usable site lets visitors find what they want without stopping to figure out how the site works. Six principles:

Follow conventions

Logo top-left, search at the top, cart top-right. Don't make users learn a new layout.

Clear visual hierarchy

The most important thing is the biggest and most prominent. Related items are grouped together.

Clearly defined areas

Break the page into obvious zones: navigation, main offer, categories, footer.

Obvious clickability

Buttons look like buttons. Links look like links. Nobody should have to hover to find out.

Eliminate distractions

Remove anything that doesn't help the visitor (or the business) reach the goal.

Format for scanning

Short paragraphs, headings, bullets, and bold key phrases.

Desktop vs. mobile: the same six rules

The slides show a craft retailer's homepage (Michaels) on both screens. On mobile, the same principles apply with much less space, so the site has to choose:

Desktop

  • Logo top-left, search bar in the center, cart top-right
  • Large hero promotion
  • Full category navigation across the top
  • Category tiles below the hero

Mobile

  • Category navigation collapses into a "hamburger" menu
  • The hero promotion shrinks
  • Store information moves up (mobile users are often nearby)
  • Some content is cut, not just resized

Framework 2

Conversion-centered design

Usability makes a page easy to use. Conversion-centered design (CCD) makes it persuasive. Every element on the page should push the visitor toward one action. Seven principles:

PrincipleThe question it asks
AttentionIs the visitor's eye drawn to the conversion goal, with few competing links?
ContextDoes the page fit where the visitor came from (the ad, email or search)?
ClarityIs the offer stated simply enough to understand in a few seconds?
CongruenceDoes every element (copy, images, layout) support the same goal?
CredibilityAre there reasons to trust you: reviews, logos, guarantees, security badges?
ClosingIs there one clear call to action that's easy to complete?
ContinuanceAfter converting, does the experience keep the relationship going (confirmation, next step)?
ExampleDropbox sign-up page, desktop and mobile
  • Attention: the sign-up form is the visual anchor of the page.
  • Clarity: a short headline explains the product in a few words.
  • Closing: one primary button. Nothing competes with it.
  • Mobile: the same principles, tighter. Two buttons create a soft hierarchy (filled primary, outlined secondary).

Discuss: does a second button on mobile help visitors, or does it leak attention away from the main goal?

Try it: attention ratio

Attention ratio compares the number of things you can click on a page to the number of things you want people to click. A dedicated landing page aims for 1:1. A homepage will always be higher, but lower is better.

Framework 3

The three questions

Within a few seconds of landing, a visitor should be able to answer three questions. If they can't answer all three on the first screen (above the fold), the page is failing.

1 · What are you offering?

Usually the headline.

2 · Why should I pick you?

Usually the subheadline: the benefit or difference.

3 · What do you want me to do next?

The call-to-action button, ideally with risk removed.

ExampleDoba (drop-shipping software)

Headline: "Drop Shipping. Simplified." answers question 1. The subhead explains why Doba is easier, answering question 2. The button offers a free 30-day trial with no credit card required, which answers question 3 and removes the risk of saying yes. On mobile, all three answers still fit on one screen.

Try it: write a hero section

Pick a business, or write your own. The checker looks for an offer, a reason to choose you, and a clear action. It's a rough check, so use your judgment too.

Framework 4

Design for segments

One page has to serve many kinds of shoppers at the same time. The conversion (or the next step toward it) should appeal to each of them. Segments can be defined on many dimensions:

Search vs. category navigation Browse vs. directed shopping Product category New vs. returning customer Online vs. in-store preference Comparison vs. brand-loyal

Framework 5

Mobile first

Design for the smallest screen first, then add features and content for larger screens. This is called progressive enhancement. The small screen forces you to decide what really matters. Going the other way (designing for desktop and shrinking it) usually produces a cluttered mobile page.

Try it: progressive enhancement

Switch screen sizes. The core content never changes. Larger screens only add things.

Hero image + priceCore: what it is, what it costs
Add to CartCore: the conversion goal
Trust badgesCore: reasons to believe
Customer reviewsCore
Technology detailsAdded on tablet
Comparison tableAdded on desktop

ExampleOwlet (baby monitor), mobile homepage

Scroll the mobile page and you see a clear sequence: hero and price, then a product configurator with trust badges, then technology details, then reviews with a sticky Add to Cart button. The punch line is the desktop site: it's hardly different from the mobile site, because it was built mobile first.

What changed since the textbook Mobile first is no longer just a design choice. Google now uses mobile-first indexing, meaning it crawls and ranks the mobile version of your site. If content is missing on mobile, Google may not see it at all.
Testing

Conversion rate optimization (CRO)

Design frameworks give you good guesses. Testing tells you which guess is right. CRO means making continual improvements to a site through repeated A/B tests: show different versions to different visitors and measure which one converts better.

CaseGrip6: one homepage, three versions
Original

Pop-up over the SHOP button

A form offering 15% off in exchange for an email address covers the SHOP button.

Variant 1

No pop-up

Hypothesis: uncovering the SHOP button will raise the conversion rate. The trade-off: fewer emails collected.

Variant 2

Pop-up moved

The email offer is still shown, but in a different place, so the SHOP button stays visible.

With three versions instead of two, this is an A/B/n test.

Setting up an A/B test

  1. Create variants of the web page
  2. Add targeting rules (who sees the test)
  3. Link to analytics to monitor results
  4. Set up objectives (what counts as a win)
  5. Start the test

Traffic weights

You don't have to split traffic evenly. Grip6 sent 60% of visitors to the original, 7% to Variant 1 and 33% to Variant 2. A small weight limits the risk if a variant performs badly, but it also means that variant collects less data.

Original 60% 7% V2 33%
What changed since the textbook The slides use Google Optimize, which Google shut down in September 2023. The same five steps apply in today's testing tools, such as Optimizely, VWO, AB Tasty, or the experiment features built into many e-commerce and email platforms.
Reading results

Which version won?

The answer depends on which number you look at. Choose a metric and watch the "winner" change.

Grip6 A/B/n test results

OriginalVariant 1Variant 2

Revenue = conversions × average order value (AOV). The slide's $5 per email reproduces its email values. Original revenue calculates to $81,600 (the slide shows $81,400, a typo; revenue per session is still $1.36).

Pick the metric that matches your business goal before the test starts. Variant 1 wins on conversion rate and revenue per session, but once email sign-ups are counted, Variant 2 delivers the most total value. And Variant 1 only got 7% of traffic, so ask whether you have enough data before you declare a winner.

Objective 2

Web page elements to decide on

Every page is a set of decisions. Each element below is a variable you can test.

🎨 Color scheme

Matches the brand, and makes the call to action stand out by contrast.

🔘 Buttons

Copy, size, color, and position. The wording often matters more than the color.

🖼️ Images

Show the product in use, or people who look like your customers.

🧭 Navigation

Fewer, clearer menu choices. Landing pages often remove navigation entirely.

🛡️ Trust symbols

Reviews, awards, press logos, security badges, guarantees.

▶️ Video

Great for demonstrations, but it slows the page and many viewers watch muted.

📝 Forms

Every extra field lowers completion. Ask only for what you need.

📞 Phone numbers

Builds trust and serves people who would rather call, especially for high-priced services.

Objectives 3 and 4

Landing pages

A landing page is the page someone arrives on after clicking an ad. The type of page should match what the person was thinking when they clicked.

Single product

One product with photos, price and an Add to Cart button. Fits an ad for a specific item.

Multi product

A category or collection. Fits an ad about a product line or a sale.

Lead generation

A form to capture contact details. Fits B2B, services, and high-priced purchases.

Subscription

Sign up for a recurring service, newsletter or free trial.

Long copy

A long, detailed page that answers every objection. Fits complex or expensive decisions.

Single purpose

One action only (download, register, RSVP), with no navigation to distract.

Ad-to-landing-page match

When the landing page doesn't deliver what the ad promised, the visitor feels lost and leaves. You paid for the click and wasted it. Check the match on these dimensions:

Headline and message

The page repeats the ad's main promise.

Offer

Any price, discount or perk in the ad appears on the page.

Look and feel

Same imagery, colors and product.

Call to action

The action the ad suggested is available right away.

Message match is where most campaigns leak. A great ad sending people to a generic homepage is money spent on traffic that doesn't convert.
Practice

Five ads, five landing pages

For each ad, decide which type of landing page it should go to and whether the real landing page matched. Commit to your answer, then reveal.

Lecture activities

CRO: the four high-impact areas

When you're deciding what to test first, start with the areas most likely to move conversions.

a · Friction

  • Make the page load fast (test it at PageSpeed Insights)
  • Reduce clutter with existence testing: remove an element and see whether results change

b · Value proposition

  • List every possible value proposition
  • Test them systematically, not just one

c · Call to action

  • CTA copy
  • Button position
  • Color and size

d · Trust

  • Awards, press, customer reviews
  • Where trust symbols sit (near the CTA beats the footer)
What changed since the textbook PageSpeed Insights now reports Google's Core Web Vitals: how fast the main content appears (LCP), how quickly the page responds to a tap (INP, which replaced FID in March 2024), and how much the layout jumps around (CLS). These measure friction the way real visitors feel it.
